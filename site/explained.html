Every data point on this site, explained

This page documents exactly where every number comes from, what it means, and what its limitations are. No hand-waving.

Open Explorer Methodology

What the graph is

A treemap — each rectangle represents one of 43 occupation groups from India's NCO 2015 classification. The size of each rectangle is proportional to how many people work in that occupation. Bigger rectangle = more workers. You can switch between 5 color layers to see different dimensions of the data.

Total Jobs: 62.4 Cr (~625 million)

This is the total Indian workforce. It comes from:

  • PLFS 2023-24 Annual Report (published by MoSPI, September 2024)
  • Statement 3: Worker Population Ratio (WPR) = 43.7% (usual status ps+ss, all ages, persons)
  • Applied to India's projected mid-2023-24 population of ~1.428 billion
  • 43.7% × 1.428B = ~624 million

The actual sum in the dataset is 625,335,360 because the sub-division percentages from Table 25 add to ~100.01% (rounding in the published tables) and ~1.4M armed forces are added separately.

"Why 1.428 billion and not census figures?"

India's last census was 2011. The 1.428B figure here is a mid-period population estimate used to convert official PLFS shares into absolute worker counts. Any current all-India workforce total requires a population estimate rather than a fresh census count.

"38% in agriculture seems too high."

It is the official PLFS figure. India's agricultural employment share actually increased in recent PLFS rounds (from 42.5% in 2017-18 to 46.1% in 2020-21 during COVID, settling to ~38% in 2023-24). This is a documented and widely-debated feature of Indian labour data, not an error.

"What about Armed Forces?"

PLFS is a civilian household survey — it does not cover military personnel. The ~1.4M figure comes from Ministry of Defence annual reports. The split across officers/NCOs/other ranks is approximate.

Important caveat: These figures cover only regular wage/salaried employees — about 21.7% of India's workforce. For context, PLFS Statement 12 reports self-employment gross earnings of ~₹13,900 over the last 30 days in April-June 2024, and Statement 11 reports casual labour earnings of ~₹433/day in April-June 2024. The pay figures on this chart do NOT represent what all workers in that occupation earn — only the salaried subset.

"Why is pay the same for all occupations within a Division?"

Because PLFS Table 50 only reports earnings at the 1-digit Division level (9 divisions), not the 2-digit sub-division level. This is a limitation of the published data, not a modelling choice. Armed Forces pay is estimated separately from MoD data since PLFS doesn't cover military.

"These pay figures seem low."

They are the official survey averages. India's median income is low by global standards. The methodology is CWS (Current Weekly Status) recall for earnings in the preceding calendar month. These are not take-home pay estimates — they are survey-reported averages.

Growth Outlook (10-Year Projected Trend)

Key disclaimer: India does not publish a BLS-style occupation forecast. This field is a directional trend extrapolation, NOT an official forecast or prediction. It is clearly labeled as such throughout the site.

How it was constructed:

  1. Observed industry-level growth from PLFS Statement 7 (industry distribution across 2021-22, 2022-23, 2023-24 — three years of published data)
  2. Mapped to NCO occupation divisions via primary industry linkage (e.g., construction industry growth → building trades occupations)
  3. Adjusted for structural factors: workforce formalization, urbanization trends, female LFPR surge (from 25.1% to 35.9% over recent PLFS rounds)
  4. AI exposure headwind applied as a dampener to routine-intensive roles
  5. Dampened for mean-reversion because post-COVID recovery growth was abnormally fast and cannot be extrapolated linearly

Range: -15% (Subsistence Farmers, code 63) to +32% (ICT Professionals, code 25). Out of 43 groups, 33 are projected growing, 9 declining, and 1 flat.

"These are just made-up numbers."

They are informed directional estimates based on three years of observed trends, adjusted for known structural factors. They are not arbitrary. But they are also not official government projections — because none exist for India. The methodology page and code comments both state this explicitly.

"+32% for ICT seems aggressive."

The +32% figure on this site is a derived directional estimate for the Code 25 bucket, not an official forecast. It reflects the project's mapping of recent PLFS industry trends and structural adjustments into occupation-level outlooks. Readers should interpret it as a scenario-style signal, not as a published government projection.

"-15% for subsistence farmers — is agriculture collapsing?"

Subsistence farming (code 63) specifically is shrinking as workers move into market-oriented agriculture (code 61) or non-farm work. Total agricultural employment is declining as a share, which is a normal structural shift seen in every developing economy. The absolute number of agricultural workers may remain stable or decline slowly.

Demand Index

Source: National Career Service (NCS) portal — vacancy counts aggregated from India's government job portal, scaled 0-100 as a relative index.

Important caveat: NCS is a government portal — it skews toward public sector and formal economy jobs. Informal sector demand (which employs ~90% of Indian workers) is not captured. This field is labeled as a "directional proxy" everywhere on the site and must not be read as total labour demand.

"NCS doesn't represent real labour demand."

Correct, and we say so. It is a signal, not a census. It indicates which occupations have relatively more or fewer formal vacancies posted on a government portal. That's useful but limited.

AI Exposure Score

Source: LLM-scored estimate — each of the 43 occupations was evaluated for how much its core tasks could be augmented or replaced by AI, calibrated specifically for the Indian labour market context.

Scale: 0-10 (0 = minimal exposure, 10 = high exposure). Current range in the dataset: 1 to 9. Each occupation also has a written rationale explaining the score, visible in the tooltip.

"An AI scoring AI exposure is circular."

This is a common and published methodology in AI research (see Eloundou et al. 2023, "GPTs are GPTs: An Early Look at the Labor Market Impact Potential of Large Language Models"). The score is clearly labeled as an "LLM-scored estimate" — not a measured statistic. The rationale for each score is shown in the tooltip so you can judge for yourself.

"Why calibrated for India specifically?"

AI exposure differs by country. A data entry clerk in the U.S. faces high AI displacement because most workflows are digital. The same role in an Indian government office with paper-based workflows faces less. The scoring accounts for India's digitization levels, infrastructure gaps, and labour cost dynamics.

Stats panel (top bar)

  • Total Jobs: Sum of all employment counts across 43 groups = 62.4 Cr
  • Layer metric: Changes with the selected color layer — shows median group pay, average outlook, average AI exposure, etc. These are straightforward aggregations over the 43 records.
  • Growing / Declining: Count of occupation groups with positive vs. negative growth outlook. Currently 33 growing, 9 declining.
  • Histogram: Distribution of employment across the current metric's range. Bar height represents total workers in that bucket (not count of occupations). Colored with the same scale as the treemap.

  • Code 35 — ICT Technicians (~6.9 lakh): IT support, hardware maintenance, network installation — the operational side of tech

The pay problem: The site shows ₹35,776/month for the Code 25 ICT Professionals bucket. This is not a software-engineer-specific salary estimate. PLFS Table 50 only reports earnings at the 1-digit Division level — so Code 25 (ICT Professionals) shares the same pay figure as Code 23 (Teaching Professionals), Code 22 (Health Professionals), and Code 26 (Legal/Social/Cultural Professionals). They are all Division 2: Professionals. This is a data granularity limitation in the official release, not an error in the chart.

"Only 49 lakh in the ICT Professionals bucket? That seems low."

This figure is for the full PLFS/NCO Code 25 bucket, not for software engineers alone and not for the entire tech industry. It comes from the PLFS occupation table and should be read as an occupation-classification estimate within this survey framework. Comparisons to industry headcounts from private-sector reports are useful context, but they are not strictly like-for-like.

"AI Exposure 8/10 — are software engineers going to be replaced?"

High exposure does not mean replacement. It means AI tools (copilots, code generation, automated testing) are highly capable at tasks software engineers do. In practice this has so far meant augmentation — engineers become more productive — rather than displacement. The score reflects task-level capability overlap, not a jobs-lost prediction. The growth outlook for this group is +32%, the highest on the chart, precisely because demand for software is accelerating even as AI changes how it's built.

Why is this 2023-24 data? Is newer data available?

This site uses the PLFS 2023-24 Annual Report (July 2023 – June 2024), published by MoSPI in September 2024. It was the most recent annual report with full NCO 2-digit occupation breakdowns at the time of building.

What has changed since

Starting January 2025, MoSPI fundamentally changed the PLFS:

  • Annual reports now follow the calendar year (Jan-Dec), not the old July-June cycle
  • Sample size increased from 12,800 to 22,692 first-stage units
  • Quarterly estimates now cover both rural and urban India (previously quarterly was urban-only)
  • Monthly bulletins are now released with national LFPR, WPR, and unemployment rate

Why we haven't updated yet: The quarterly and monthly bulletins publish aggregate indicators (LFPR, WPR, unemployment rate) but may not include the full NCO 2-digit occupation breakdown (Table 25 equivalent) that we need to size the treemap rectangles. The Calendar Year 2024 annual report is the most promising candidate for an update. We are evaluating whether it contains occupation-level distribution data at the granularity this visualization requires.

"Is the 2023-24 data outdated?"

Structural labour market composition does not change dramatically year-to-year. Agriculture was ~38% in 2023-24; it is unlikely to have shifted by more than 1-2 percentage points since. The relative sizes and rankings of occupation groups remain broadly valid. That said, we plan to update to CY2024 data once we confirm it has the occupation-level tables we need.

"The latest quarterly data shows different numbers."

Quarterly bulletins use Current Weekly Status (CWS), which typically shows lower employment and higher unemployment than the Usual Status (ps+ss) measure used in annual reports. These are different measurement approaches, not contradictions. This site uses Usual Status because it captures the structural picture better.

What this is and what it is not

This is: A research-grade visualization that combines official Indian survey data (PLFS 2023-24) with clearly labeled proxy and estimated fields. Every source is cited. Every limitation is documented. The code is open source.

This is not: An official government publication, a census, or a prediction of future employment levels. It does not claim to capture informal sector dynamics, state-level variation, or individual career outcomes.
